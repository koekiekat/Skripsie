\mysection{Bryde's Whales}{Bryde's Whales}\label{sec:bryde-explained}

Bryde's Whales predominently live in tropical or subtropical environments across the world. 
The different call types, rates, frequencies and harmonics of these whales vary depending on geographical location of 
the colony. Generally Bryde's Whales have calls containg frequencies from as low as 
42 Hz to as high as 1kHz. 

In the dataset used for this project there were four main call types present. These are referred to as 
single-tones, multi-tones, burst-tonals and tonal downsweeps. 

The single-tone calls were charecterized by a singular frequency at around 42 Hz. Multi-tones also contained 
the 42 Hz signel, but there were one or two other frequencies above this with a maximum frequency componet 
at about 100 Hz. Burst-tonals also has the multiple frequency characteristic of the multi-tones, however the lower frequencies, 42 - 200 Hz, 
were held for longer than the higher frequencies, 200 - 500 Hz. 
The tonal downsweep was the only call type not characterized by the 42 Hz frequency. It was a sweeping of 
frequencies through time starting at around 200 Hz and ending at around 100 Hz. 

In accordance with original detected calls the average call length of each call type was around 0.3 seconds 
with a maximum duration of about 2.5 seconds. In other studies of Bryde's Whales calls in different geographical areas a maximum 
duration of arount 7.0 seconds was identified.\cite{bryde_whales_SA}\cite{bryde_geographicly}

Decisions made in the project pertaining to frequencies and durations falling within the detection limits were made based on the above information.

\mysection{Dynamic Time Warping}{Dynamic Time Warping}\label{sec:dynamic-time-warping}
DTW is an algorithm for detecting the similarity of two signals, $X = [x_1, x_2, \dots, x_n]$ and $Y = [y_1, y_2, \dots, y_m]$. This measure of similarity is achieved by calculating an optimal 
alignment cost between the two signals of length $N \in\mathbb{N}$  and $M \in\mathbb{N}$ respectively. In terms of feature vectors such as STFT or MFCC 
this means that each frame making up one signal is compared to each frame making up another signal. Using an alignment cost the best alignment for each 
frame in one to the other signal is chosen and an overall DTW cost is calculated to show how similar the overall signals are. Figure~\ref{fig:vector_dtw} shows that 
such an optimal alignment path may mean that a single frame in signal X may produce the lowest cost against more than one frame in signal Y.\cite{dtw_basic}\cite{dtw_kamper}

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.8\linewidth]{figures/background/vector_dtw.png}
    \caption{An example of the optimal warping path between two vector features, X with length N = 4 and Y with length M = 6}\label{fig:vector_dtw}
\end{figure}

The overall cost needed to determine how similar the two signals are is calculated via a cost matrix. Cost matrix, $C_{N+1, M+1}$ for signals X and Y, 
is initialised such that $C_{i, 0} = \infty$ for i = 1 to M, $C_{0, j} = \infty$ for j = 1 to M and $C_{0, 0} = 0$. 

The formula used to fill each cell of the cost matrix is:

\begin{equation}
C_{i, j} = \text{dist}(x_i, y_j) + \text{Q} \qquad \text{with} \quad \text{Q} = \min\begin{cases}
    C_{i-1, j-1} & \text{(match)} \\
    C_{i-1, j}   & \text{(insertion)} \\
    C_{i, j-1}   & \text{(deletion)}
\end{cases}
\end{equation}

$dist(x_i, y_j)$ represents the difference between $X_i$ and $Y_j$ as a distance. For vector signals this distance is calculated between each frame i 
of signal X and each fram j of signal Y. This can be done using various different techniques including cosine, euclidean or dot product similarity.
For this project only the cosine similarity and euclidean distance metrics were used.\cite{dtw_kamper}

Euclidean distance is sensitive to the magnitude and direction of the vectors for which it is calculated. In basic terms it is the absolute difference between 
two vectors.\cite{vector_sim}

\begin{equation}
    d(\mathbf{x_i}, \mathbf{y_j}) = \sqrt{(\mathbf{x_i}_1 - \mathbf{y_j}_1)^2 + (\mathbf{x_i}_2 - \mathbf{y_j}_2)^2 + \dots + (\mathbf{x_i}_n - \mathbf{y_j}_n)^2}
    \label{eq:euclidean} 
\end{equation}

Cosine similarity is only sensitive to the direction of the vectors for which it is calculated. In basic terms it is the angle between the two vectors.\cite{vector_sim}

\begin{equation}
    \cos(\mathbf{x_i}, \mathbf{y_j}) = \frac{\mathbf{x_i} \cdot \mathbf{y_j}}{\lVert\mathbf{x_i}\rVert \, \lVert\mathbf{y_j}\rVert}
    \label{eq:cosine} 
\end{equation}

Q is the minimum value of three possible options namely match, insertion or deletion. Match is the value in the matrix cell diagonally down and left 
from the current cell for which the cost is being calculated. Insertion is the value in the matrix cell directly below the current cell and deletion is 
the matrix cell directly to the left of the current cell.

The cost which is then calculated for the current cell $C_{i, j}$ is a summation of the distance between the feature frames i and j in signals X and Y respectively 
as well as the lowest accumlated cost up until that point. This means that the final cell reached within the cost matrix, $C_{N+1, M+1}$ will contain the 
lowest overall cost for the similarity of signals X and Y. The lower the final cost, the higher the similarity between the two signals. The example shown in Figure~\ref{fig:cost_matrix} 
is shown if X and Y are chosen to be scalar signals, however the same logic is used for when X and Y are vectors.\cite{dtw_kamper}

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.6\linewidth]{figures/background/cost_matrix.png}
    \caption{An example for the three possible options for min as well as an example of the optimal warp path found for signals X and Y assuming X=[0, 2, 1, 0] 
    and Y=[0, 0, 0.5, 2, 1, 0]}\label{fig:cost_matrix}
\end{figure}

The general case of DTW finds global alignment between the two signals. This alignment is found using the warping path.
The warping path is the path of the best alignment between signals X and Y within the cost matrix, as shown in Figure~\ref{fig:cost_matrix}.
This path, W, is a sequence of matrix co-ordinates, (i, j), with a total length k.\cite{dtw_boundaries}

The boundary condition for global alignment ensures the final path starts and ends with the start and endframes of each respective signal. 
\begin{equation}
    i_1 = 1, i_k = N \quad \text{and} \quad j_1 = 1, j_k = M
\end{equation}

The monotonicity condition ensures that each new point in the warp path is a step forward in time from the previous step.
\begin{equation}
    i_{t-1} \le i_t \quad \text{and} \quad j_{t-1} \le j_t
\end{equation}

The continuity condition ensures the step size of the warping path is only one unit in time.
\begin{equation}
    i_t - i_{t-1} \le 1 \quad \text{and} \quad j_t - j_{t-1} \le 1
\end{equation}

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.6\linewidth]{figures/literature/sub_dtw.png}
    \caption{Example of a subsequence DTW alignment vetween two signal $X = [x_1, x_2, x_3, x_4]$ and $Y = [y_1. y_2, y_3, y_4, y_5, y_6, y_7, y_8, y_9, y_10]$}\label{fig:sub_dtw}
\end{figure}

Subsequence DTW is a version of the DTW algorithm that alters the boundary condition such that global alignment is no longer necessary, 
instead the entirety of signal X is now matched with a subsequence of signal Y.
The new boundary condition allows the warping path to start and end at anypoint within the first and final row of the matrix respectively.
\begin{equation}
    i_1 = 1,\; i_k = N \quad \text{and} \quad 1 \le j_1 \le j_k \le M
\end{equation}

In global alignment the final cell in the cost matrix (N+1, M+1) is considered the overall optimal cost for those two signals. With subsequence DTW the entire final row 
of the matrix is checked to find the lowest cost. The position of that cost shows where the end point of the alignment detected via subsequence DTW is.\cite{dtw_subsequence}

\mysection{Feature Extraction Methods}{Feature Extraction Methods}\label{sec:feature-extraction-methods-background}


\mysubsection{Short Time Fourier Transform}{Short Time Fourier Transform}\label{sec:short-time-fourier-transform}
The STFT is used to analyse the change in frequency of a signal over time. The STFT of a segment of audio is calculated 
by multiplying the signal in the time domain, $x(t)$ with a window $w(t-\tau)$ which is sweeped across said signal. 
The Fourier Transform of each window is calculated and used to determine the frequency information for that window. Thus, the STFT provides the change in frequency
for a segment of audio throughout time.\cite{Det_Tech}\cite{stft_kamper}
The mathematical formulation of STFT is:
  \begin{equation}
    X(\tau, \omega) = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} x(t)\, w(t - \tau)\, e^{-j\omega t}\, dt,
  \end{equation}

where $\tau$ is the position of the window as it shifts through time at step-size $H$ and $\omega$ is the frequency being analysed.

The frequency resolution of the STFT is given as:
\begin{equation}
    resolution = \frac{f_s}{L}
    \label{eq:freq_resolution} 
\end{equation}

where $f_s$ is the sampling frequency of the signal and L is the length of the window overwhich the FT is taken. 

This means that the STFT has a trade-off between time and frequency resolution. 

If L is kept short, time resolution is high since smaller segments of time are being used for one FT analysis. However, frequency resolution is then decreased 
since too little time passes for the FT to distinguish between frequencies spaced closer together.

If L is kept long, time resolution drops. This means that fast changes in frequency will be missed. However, frequency resolution increases 
and it becomes easier to distinguish between differrent frequency bands.

Window overlap is another consideration when using STFT. Increased overlap creates a smoother representation of the changes 
in frequency occuring over time since information at the edges of the window is no longer lost. The trade-off with overlap is the increase in computational complexity 
that will occur due to smaller and thus more windows of a signal being analysed.\cite{dtw_basic}\cite{stft_kamper}


\mysubsection{Mel-Frequency Cepstral Coefficients}{Mel-Frequency Cepstral Coefficients}\label{sec:mel-frequency-cepstral-coefficients}
MFCC is a common choice for feature extraction when working on aucoustic data. This is because it is set up to percieve frequencies as they are percieved by the human ear. That means 
the lower frequencies are given a higher resolution than the high frequencies.\cite{mel_kamper}
Features are initially extracted using the FFT. After this they are passed through a Mel-scale Filter bank. The formula for the mel-frequency calculation is:

\begin{equation}
    Mel(f) = 1127 \ln\left(1 + \frac{f}{700}\right)
\end{equation}

 with $Mel(f)$ as the frequency value on the mel scale and f as the actual frequency in Hz.\cite{Det_Tech}

 This bank is made up 20 to 40 triangular filters which are placed 
,smallest width to largest width according to the mel scale, along the frequency spectrum. The standard amount of triangular filters chosen is 26. This results in 26 coefficients for the entire range of 
frequencies being analysed.\cite{mel_med}\cite{mel_kamper}

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.6\linewidth]{figures/literature/mel.png}
    \caption{The mel scale filter bank is made up of 20 to 40 triangular filters arranged according to the mel scale.}\label{fig:mel}
\end{figure}

The log of these coefficients is then taken and finally the Discrete cosine transform (DCT) is used to decorrelate the filter bank coefficients.
The final number of cepstral coefficients returned by the DCT is typically 12 or 13 since the most important information is kept there. The formula use to perform the DCT is:

\begin{equation}
    MFCC_i = \sum_{k=1}^{K} X_k \cos\left(\frac{i(k - 0.5)\pi}{K}\right), \quad i = 1, 2, \dots, K
\end{equation}

with i as the index of the current coefficient, K as the total number of triangular filters used, k as the specific filter bank at that point in time and $X_k$ as the energy after log 
was applied to the mel coefficients.\cite{Det_Tech}

The first and second derivatives, often referred to as deltas, of the cepstral coefficients are often used in feature extraction. They show the change in frequency between cepstral coefficients. This can 
be vital information when analysing how frequency changes across time since the MFCC process tends to lose this information. The formula for the derivatives is:

\begin{equation}
    x^{(d)}_t = \frac{\sum_{m=-M}^{M} m \cdot x^{(d-1)}_{t+m}}{\sum_{m=-M}^{M} m^2}
\end{equation}

with t as the specific point in time, d as the order of derivative and m as the frame offset.\cite{deltas}\cite{mel_delta}\cite{melderv_calcs}

\mysubsection{Linear Predictive Coding}{Linear Predictive Coding}\label{sec:linear-predictive-coding}
Linear Predicitve Coding (LPC) is a straightforward mathematical method commonly used for feature extraction. LPC focuses on the method of 
approximating a sample of a speech signal using a combination of n samples from the past. This means that for each sample for which there are 
p available samples before it an approximation is made in the form of the equation:

\begin{equation}
    \tilde{s}(n) = \alpha_1s(n-1) + \alpha_2s(n-2) +\cdots+ \alpha_ps(n-p)
\end{equation}

where $\tilde{s}(n)$ is the approximated speech sample and $\alpha_1, \alpha_2, \ldots, \alpha_p$ are the coefficients for that approximation. The p coefficients used are constant over all 
samples within the given frame.

For each sample with an approximation an error is calculated between the real sample and the approximated sample.

\begin{equation}
    e_n = s(n) - \tilde{s}(n)
\end{equation}

The sum of the squares of all these errors is minimised to find the p constant coefficients. The equation for this is:

\begin{equation}
    E_n = \sum_m e_n^2(m)
\end{equation}

where m is the sample for which the error $e_n(m)$ was calculated.

The feature extracted via this method is is the p LPC coefficients which were calculated to minimise the mean square error. 
These coefficients tend to be unstable, therefore Cepstral Coefficients can be calculated from these raw LPC coefficients. 
Cepstral Coefficients are the coefficients of the FT representation of the magnitude spectrum and are more reliable than the LPC. 

LPCC can be calculated from the LPC results through recursion. The formula for that is:

\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
c_0 &= \ln \sigma^2 \\
c_m &= a_m + \sum_{k=1}^{m-1} \left(\frac{k}{m}\right) c_k a_{m-k}, \qquad 1 \le m \le p \\
c_m &= \sum_{k=1}^{m-1} \left(\frac{k}{m}\right) c_k a_{m-k}, \qquad m > p,
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}

where $\sigma^2$ is the LPC gain term.

The final feature extracted using LPCC contains p coefficients for a single frame with p=0 containing the magnitude information.\cite{lpc}\cite{Det_Tech}

\mysubsection{Classification of Killer Whale Vocalizations using DTW}{Classification of Killer Whale Vocalizations using DTW}\label{sec:killer-dtw}
An article, ``Automatic classification of killer whale vocalizations using
dynamic time warping'', written by Judith, C. and Patrrick J.O. Miller evaluated 
four different variations of the \ac{DTW} algortithm to classify biphonic killer whale vocalizations using fundamental frequency contours. 

Due to the biphonic nature of these calls the Low-Frequency Compinent and High-Frequency Component for each 
call was extracted for seperate analysis. Four different variations of the DTW algorithm with various different constraints 
were used to evluate against each other. 

The final result of the paper was that the method referred to as ``Sakoe-Chiba'' on the LPC performed the best with 
a 90\% agreement with the labeles selected by human classifiers. 

While this study did show that the use of DTW in bioacustic classification and thus is a good option 
for bioacustic analysis, it did not consider the ability of DTW to detect call in a continuos segmetn of bioacustic data 
as all calls which were classified had already been selected and segmented to the pitch contours of the vocalization. 
This study also did not consider the impact of low frequency analysis using DTW as killer whale vocalizations do not tend to 
fall below 500 Hz.\cite{killer-whale}

\mysubsection{Detection of Template-Based Detection Algorithms for Bryde's Whales}{Detection of Template-Based Detection Algorithms for Bryde's Whales}\label{sec:bryde-dtw-lpc}

The article, ``Analysis of Template-Base Detection Algorithms for Inshore Bryde's 
Whale Short Pulse Calls'', analysed two template based detection algorithms to detect short pulse calls for 
Bryde's Whales in Fasle Bay, South Africa. The two methods tested were DTW and LPC. In their analysis they 
used two seperate sample sets with different SNR values to also evaluate the performance of these techniques in 
the presence of noise. The input features for these models were the time-domain representation of the signals. 

The final results of the article found that both LPC and DTW performed well in high and low SNR segments. LPC 
performed the overall best in low noise segments.

This project provides insight into the use of template based detection systems for Bryde's 
Whales. This is relevant since DTW which is handled in this article is the chosen detection mechanism 
for this project. While LPC is also used in the article, its use is vastly different to the use within this 
project. LPC is used as a detection algorithm and tested on its ability to act as such. In this project LPC is not used 
as a detection algorithm, but instead as a feature extraction method. The detection method focuses on the error component of 
the LPC equation while the feature extraction method focuses on the coefficients calculated for the approximation of the real signal. 
Another difference between this project and the article is that this project exclusively uses the time domain representation of the 
signal as input. In this project even when LPC is used as a feture extraction method it is converted to LPCC thus all the 
features used for the DTW algorithm are describing frequency domain characteristics.\cite{temp-based-bryde}

\mysubsection{Detection of real-time fin and blue whale calls}{Detection of real-time fin and blue whale calls}\label{sec:real-time}

The article, ``Automatic recognition of fin and blue whale calls for real-time
monitoring in the St. Lawrence'', testing detetcion models on real-time data for fin and blue whales. 
The detection methods used was DTW and Vector Quantization (VQ). This article focused on low frequency whale calls. 

Instananeous frequency, speed and acceleration was extracted as features. 
It was found that due to low frequency components from background elements such as ships 
the detection of False Positives increased.

In terms of this project the article shows how low frequency detections can be 
affected by low frequency background noise. This article also considered real-time 
audio detection which is much closer in similarity to detection within a large audio file 
such as what was done in this project. This article did not use the same feature vectors 
as the project and did not consider their effect on DTW performance.\cite{dtw-vq}

\mysubsection{Hidden Markov Model for Bryde's Whale short pulse calls}{Hidden Markov Model for Bryde's Whale short pulse calls}\label{sec:hmm-bryde}

This paper compares a time domain feature extraction technique used for HMM against MFCC and 
LPC, frequency domain techniques. The model is used for detection of Bryde's Whale calls in 
False Bay, South Africa.

These three HMM models are used on 2 hours of continuos recordings to detect these calls. This 
is similar to the continuos recording detection done in this project.

The MAP-HMM model performed the best with MFCC performing second best and LPC performing the worst. 
It was found that the MFCC and LPC models were sensitive to background noise.

While this does test detection on a continuos segment of audio, this paper focuses on HMM detection and 
not DTW. It also does test MFCC feature extraction method as well as the LPC method. Considering the fact that 
the LPC features were not converted to LPCC this does not fully align with this project.\cite{hmm}

\mysubsection{Speech Recognition using DTW}{Speech Recognition using DTW}\label{sec: speech-dtw}

This goal of this paper was to analyse the effectiveness of Dynamic Time Warping in matching speech 
patterns. Variabilities were included into the study via pronounciation timing, volume and speaker identity. 

Features were extracted from the recorded audio. Some audio was used as templates and others as test data. 
DTW was then used to test which of the variations impacted calculated DTW distances the most. The main tests 
were keeping the pronounciation time the same with the same volume, different pronounciation time with the same volume and same pronounciation time with different volume. 

It was determined that DTW was an effective pattern recognition algorithm and that pronounciation timing had 
a larger impact on DTW alignment than volume.

This study also uses DTW to recognise patterns in audio, however this focuses on speech recognition and the effects of variances 
abilities in that recognition. It does not focus on different feature vectors or detection of low frequency whale calls in a continuos 
recording.\cite{speech}
