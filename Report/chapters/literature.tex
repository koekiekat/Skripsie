\mysection{Bryde's Whales}{Bryde's Whales}\label{sec:bryde-explained}

Bryde's whales predominantly live in tropical or subtropical environments across the 
world. The different call types, rates, frequencies and harmonics of these whales vary 
depending on the geographical location of the colony. Generally Bryde's whales have calls 
containing frequencies from as low as 42 Hz to as high as 1 kHz~\cite{bryde_geographicly}.

In the dataset used for this project there were four main call types present. These are 
referred to as single-tones, multi-tones, burst-tonals and tonal downsweeps. 

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.8\linewidth]{figures/literature/call_types.png}
    \caption{Examples of how each call type looks: single-tone, multi-tone, burst-tonal and tonal downsweep. The spectrograms for 
    each call type were printed based on labeled calls from the dataset~\cite{gunther_calls}.} \label{fig:call_types}
\end{figure}

As explained by Faustmann et al.~\cite{bryde_whales_SA} The single-tone calls were characterised by a 
singular frequency at around 42 Hz. 
Multi-tones also contained the 42 Hz frequency, but there were one or two other frequencies 
above this with a maximum frequency component at about 200 Hz. Burst-tonals also have 
the multiple frequency characteristic of the multi-tones, however the lower frequencies, 
42~-~200 Hz, were held for longer than the higher frequencies, 200~-~500 Hz. The tonal 
downsweep was the only call type not characterised by the 42 Hz frequency. It was a 
sweeping of frequencies through time starting at around 200 Hz and ending at around 100 
Hz.

In accordance with the original detected calls the average call length of each call type was 
around 0.3 seconds with a maximum duration of about 2.5 seconds. In other studies of Bryde's whale calls in different geographical areas, a maximum duration of around 7.0 
seconds was identified by Faustmann et al.~\cite{bryde_whales_SA} and Oleson et al.~\cite{bryde_geographicly}.

Decisions made in the project pertaining to frequencies and durations falling within 
the detection limits were made based on the above information.

\mysection{Dynamic Time Warping}{Dynamic Time Warping}\label{sec:dynamic-time-warping}
Dynamic time warping (DTW) is an algorithm for detecting the similarity of two signals, $X = [x_1, x_2, \dots, x_n]$ and 
$Y = [y_1, y_2, \dots, y_m]$. This measure of similarity is achieved by calculating an optimal 
alignment cost between the two signals of length $N \in\mathbb{N}$  and $M \in\mathbb{N}$ respectively. In terms 
of feature vectors such as the short time Fourier transform (STFT), mel-frequency cepstral coefficients (MFCC) or linear predictive coding coefficients (LPCC) 
this means that each frame making up one 
signal is compared to each frame making up another signal. Using an alignment cost the 
best alignment for each frame in one signal to the other signal is chosen and an overall DTW 
cost is calculated to show how similar the overall signals are. Figure~\ref{fig:vector_dtw} shows, as 
explained by M\"uller and Zalkow~\cite{dtw_basic} and Kamper~\cite{dtw_kamper}, that such 
an optimal alignment path may mean that a single frame in signal X may produce the 
lowest cost against more than one frame in signal Y and vice versa.

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.8\linewidth]{figures/background/vector_dtw.png}
    \caption{An example of the optimal warping path between two vector features, X with length N = 4 and Y with length M = 6}\label{fig:vector_dtw}
\end{figure}

The overall cost needed to determine how similar the two signals are is calculated via a 
cost matrix. Cost matrix, $C_{N+1, M+1}$ for signals X and Y, is initialised such that $C_{i, 0} = \infty$ 
for i = 1 to N, $C_{0, j} = \infty$ for j = 1 to M and $C_{0, 0} = 0$. 

The formula used to fill each cell of the cost matrix is:

\begin{equation}
C_{i, j} = \text{dist}(x_i, y_j) + \text{Q} \qquad \text{with} \quad \text{Q} = \min\begin{cases}
    C_{i-1, j-1} & \text{(match)} \\
    C_{i-1, j}   & \text{(insertion)} \\
    C_{i, j-1}   & \text{(deletion)}
\end{cases}
\end{equation}

where $dist(x_i, y_j)$ represents the difference between $X_i$ and $Y_j$ as a distance. For vector signals 
this distance is calculated between each frame i of signal X and each frame j of signal Y. 
According to Kamper~\cite{dtw_kamper}, this can be done using various different techniques including cosine, Euclidean or dot 
product similarity. For this project only the cosine distance and Euclidean distance 
metrics were used.

Euclidean distance is sensitive to the magnitude and direction of the vectors for which 
it is calculated. In basic terms, as described by Schwaber-Cohen~\cite{vector_sim}, it is the absolute difference between two vectors.

\begin{equation}
    d(\mathbf{x_i}, \mathbf{y_j}) = \sqrt{(\mathbf{x_i}_1 - \mathbf{y_j}_1)^2 + (\mathbf{x_i}_2 - \mathbf{y_j}_2)^2 + \dots + (\mathbf{x_i}_n - \mathbf{y_j}_n)^2}
    \label{eq:euclidean} 
\end{equation}

Cosine similarity is only sensitive to the direction of the vectors for which it is calculated. 
In basic terms, as described by Schwaber-Cohen~\cite{vector_sim}, it is the angle between the two vectors.

\begin{equation}
    \cos(\mathbf{x_i}, \mathbf{y_j}) = \frac{\mathbf{x_i} \cdot \mathbf{y_j}}{\lVert\mathbf{x_i}\rVert \, \lVert\mathbf{y_j}\rVert}
    \label{eq:cosine} 
\end{equation}

Since DTW minimises the cost between frames, the cosine similarity cannot be used directly as the distance, 
because a higher similarity must give a lower cost. Instead the cosine distance is used, which is defined in 
the SciPy documentation~\cite{scipy_dist} as:

\begin{equation}
    d_{\cos}(\mathbf{x_i}, \mathbf{y_j}) = 1 - \cos(\mathbf{x_i}, \mathbf{y_j})
    \label{eq:cosine_distance} 
\end{equation}

A cosine distance of 0 therefore means that the two vectors point in the same direction.

Q is the minimum value of three possible options namely match, insertion or deletion. 
Match is the value in the matrix cell diagonally down and left from the current cell for 
which the cost is being calculated. Insertion is the value in the matrix cell directly below 
the current cell and deletion is the matrix cell directly to the left of the current cell.

The cost which is then calculated for the current cell, $C_{i, j}$, is a summation of the distance 
between the feature frames i and j in signals X and Y respectively as well as the lowest 
accumulated cost up until that point. This means that the final cell reached within the 
cost matrix, $C_{N+1, M+1}$, will contain the lowest overall cost for the similarity of signals X 
and Y. The lower the final cost, the higher the similarity between the two signals. The 
example shown in Figure~\ref{fig:cost_matrix} assumes that X and Y are scalar signals, however, as 
explained by Kamper~\cite{dtw_kamper}, the same logic is used when X and Y are vectors.

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.6\linewidth]{figures/background/cost_matrix.png}
    \caption{An example for the three possible options for min as well as an example of the optimal warp path found for signals X and Y assuming X=[0, 2, 1, 0] 
    and Y=[0, 0, 0.5, 2, 1, 0]}\label{fig:cost_matrix}
\end{figure}

The general case of DTW finds global alignment between the two signals. This 
alignment is found using the warping path as described by Alizadeh~\cite{dtw_boundaries}. The warping path is the path of the best 
alignment between signals X and Y within the cost matrix, as shown in Figure~\ref{fig:cost_matrix}. 
This path, W, is a sequence of matrix co-ordinates, (i, j), with a total length k.

The boundary condition for global alignment ensures the final path starts and ends 
with the start and end frames of each respective signal. 

\begin{equation}
    i_1 = 1, i_k = N \quad \text{and} \quad j_1 = 1, j_k = M
\end{equation}

The monotonicity condition ensures that each new point in the warping path is a step 
forward in time from the previous step.

\begin{equation}
    i_{t-1} \le i_t \quad \text{and} \quad j_{t-1} \le j_t
\end{equation}

The continuity condition ensures the step size of the warping path is only one unit in 
time.

\begin{equation}
    i_t - i_{t-1} \le 1 \quad \text{and} \quad j_t - j_{t-1} \le 1
\end{equation}

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.6\linewidth]{figures/literature/sub_dtw.png}
    \caption{Example of a subsequence DTW alignment between two signals $X = [x_1, x_2, x_3, x_4]$ and $Y = [y_1, y_2, y_3, y_4, y_5, y_6, y_7, y_8, y_9, y_{10}]$}\label{fig:sub_dtw}
\end{figure}

Subsequence DTW is a version of the DTW algorithm that alters the boundary 
condition such that global alignment is no longer necessary. Instead the entirety of signal 
X is now matched with a subsequence of signal Y. The new boundary condition allows the 
warping path to start and end at any point within the first and final row of the matrix 
respectively.

\begin{equation}
    i_1 = 1,\ i_k = N \quad \text{and} \quad 1 \le j_1 \le j_k \le M
\end{equation}

In global alignment the final cell in the cost matrix, $C_{N+1, M+1}$, is considered the 
overall optimal cost for those two signals. As described by M\"uller and Zalkow~\cite{dtw_subsequence}, 
with subsequence DTW the entire final row of 
the matrix is checked to find the lowest cost. The position of that cost shows where the 
end point of the alignment detected via subsequence DTW is.

\mysection{Feature Extraction Methods}{Feature Extraction Methods}\label{sec:feature-extraction-methods-background}


\mysubsection{Short Time Fourier Transform}{Short Time Fourier Transform}\label{sec:short-time-fourier-transform}
According to Usman et al.~\cite{Det_Tech} and Kamper~\cite{stft_kamper}, the short time Fourier transform (STFT) is used to analyse the change in frequency of a signal over time. The STFT of 
a segment of audio is calculated by multiplying the signal in the time domain, $x(t)$ with 
a window $w(t-\tau)$ which is swept across said signal. The Fourier transform (FT) of each 
window is calculated and used to determine the frequency information for that window. 
Thus, the STFT provides the change in frequency for a segment of audio throughout 
time.

The mathematical formulation of STFT is:

  \begin{equation}
    X(\tau, \omega) = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} x(t)\, w(t - \tau)\, e^{-j\omega t}\, dt,
  \end{equation}

where $\tau$ is the position of the window as it shifts through time at step-size $H$ and $\omega$ is 
the frequency being analysed.

The frequency resolution of the STFT is given as:
\begin{equation}
    resolution = \frac{f_s}{L}
    \label{eq:freq_resolution} 
\end{equation}

where $f_s$ is the sampling frequency of the signal and L is the length of the window over which the FT is taken. 

This means that the STFT has a trade-off between time and frequency resolution. 

If L is kept short, time resolution is high since smaller segments of time are being used 
for one FT analysis. However, frequency resolution is then decreased since too little time 
passes for the FT to distinguish between frequencies spaced closer together.

If L is kept long, time resolution drops. This means that fast changes in frequency will 
be missed. However, frequency resolution increases and it becomes easier to distinguish 
between different frequency bands.

Window overlap is another consideration when using STFT. Increased overlap creates 
a smoother representation of the changes in frequency occurring over time since information 
at the edges of the window is no longer lost.


\mysubsection{Mel-Frequency Cepstral Coefficients}{Mel-Frequency Cepstral Coefficients}\label{sec:mel-frequency-cepstral-coefficients}
Mel-frequency cepstral coefficients (MFCC) is a common choice for feature extraction when working on acoustic data. This is 
because, as explained by Kamper~\cite{mel_kamper}, it is set up to perceive frequencies as they are perceived by the human ear. That 
means the lower frequencies are given a higher resolution than the high frequencies.
Features are initially extracted using the fast Fourier transform (FFT). After this they are passed through a 
mel-scale filter bank. The formula for the mel-frequency calculation given by Usman et al.~\cite{Det_Tech} is:

\begin{equation}
    Mel(f) = 1127 \ln\left(1 + \frac{f}{700}\right)
\end{equation}

 with $Mel(f)$ as the frequency value on the mel scale and f as the actual frequency in 
 Hz.

This bank is made up of 20 to 40 triangular filters which are placed, smallest width to 
largest width according to the mel scale, along the frequency spectrum. According to 
Singh~\cite{mel_med} and Kamper~\cite{mel_kamper}, the standard 
amount of triangular filters chosen is 26. This results in 26 coefficients for the entire range 
of frequencies being analysed.

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.6\linewidth]{figures/literature/mel.png}
    \caption{The mel scale filter bank is made up of 20 to 40 triangular filters arranged according to the mel scale.}\label{fig:mel}
\end{figure}

The log of these coefficients is then taken and finally the discrete cosine transform 
(DCT) is used to decorrelate the filter bank coefficients. That means any patterns linking different coefficients to each 
other are removed such that the coefficients become independent. 
The final number of cepstral 
coefficients returned by the DCT is typically 12 or 13 since the most important information 
is kept there. The formula used by Usman et al.~\cite{Det_Tech} to perform the DCT is:

\begin{equation}
    MFCC_i = \sum_{k=1}^{K} X_k \cos\left(\frac{i(k - 0.5)\pi}{K}\right), \quad i = 1, 2, \dots, K
\end{equation}

with i as the index of the current coefficient, K as the total number of triangular filters 
used, k as the specific filter bank at that point in time and $X_k$ as the energy after log was 
applied to the mel coefficients.

As explained by DataMListic~\cite{deltas}\cite{mel_delta}, the first and second derivatives, often referred to as deltas, of the cepstral coefficients 
are often used in feature extraction. They show the change of each cepstral coefficient across frames. This can be vital information when analysing how frequency changes across 
time since the MFCC process tends to lose this information. The formula for the derivatives, 
as given by Majeed et al.~\cite{melderv_calcs}, 
is:

\begin{equation}
    x^{(d)}_t = \frac{\sum_{m=-M}^{M} m \cdot x^{(d-1)}_{t+m}}{\sum_{m=-M}^{M} m^2}
\end{equation}

with t as the specific point in time, d as the order of derivative, M as the number of frames on either side of t 
and m as the frame offset.

\mysubsection{Linear Predictive Coding Coefficients}{Linear Predictive Coding Coefficients}\label{sec:linear-predictive-coding}
As explained by Rabiner and Juang~\cite{lpc} and Usman et al.~\cite{Det_Tech}, linear predictive coding (LPC) is a straightforward mathematical method commonly 
used for feature extraction. LPC focuses on the method of approximating a sample of a 
speech signal using a combination of $p$ samples from the past. This means that for each 
sample for which there are $p$ available samples before it an approximation is made in the 
form of the equation:

\begin{equation}
    \tilde{s}(n) = \alpha_1s(n-1) + \alpha_2s(n-2) +\cdots+ \alpha_ps(n-p)
\end{equation}

where $\tilde{s}(n)$ is the approximated speech sample and $\alpha_1, \alpha_2, \ldots, \alpha_p$ are the coefficients 
for that approximation. The $p$ coefficients used are constant over all samples within the 
given frame.

For each sample with an approximation an error is calculated between the real sample 
and the approximated sample.

\begin{equation}
    e_n = s(n) - \tilde{s}(n)
\end{equation}

The sum of the squares of all these errors is minimised to find the $p$ constant coefficients. 
The equation for this is:

\begin{equation}
    E_n = \sum_m e_n^2(m)
\end{equation}

where $m$ is the sample for which the error $e_n(m)$ was calculated.

The feature extracted via this method is made up of the $p$ LPC coefficients which were calculated 
to minimise the mean square error. These coefficients tend to be unstable, therefore cepstral 
coefficients can be calculated from these raw LPC coefficients giving linear predictive coding coefficients (LPCC). 
LPCC gives 
the coefficients of the Fourier transform (FT) representation of the magnitude spectrum and is more reliable 
than the LPC representation. 

LPCC can be calculated from the LPC results through recursion. The formula for that 
is:

\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
c_0 &= \ln \sigma^2 \\
c_m &= a_m + \sum_{k=1}^{m-1} \left(\frac{k}{m}\right) c_k a_{m-k}, \qquad 1 \le m \le p \\
c_m &= \sum_{k=1}^{m-1} \left(\frac{k}{m}\right) c_k a_{m-k}, \qquad m > p,
\label{eq:lpcc}
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}

where $\sigma^2$ is the LPC gain term.

The final feature vector extracted using LPCC contains $p$ coefficients for a single frame, with the zeroth coefficient, $c_0$, containing the magnitude information.

\mysection{Similar Studies}{Similar Studies}\label{sec:similar_studies}

\mysubsection{Classification of Killer Whale Vocalisations using DTW}{Classification of Killer Whale Vocalisations using DTW}\label{sec:killer-dtw}
Brown and Miller evaluated four different variations of the \ac{DTW} algorithm to classify biphonic killer whale 
vocalisations using fundamental frequency contours (see~\cite{killer-whale}). 

Due to the biphonic nature of these calls the low-frequency component and high-frequency component for each call was extracted for separate analysis. Four different 
variations of the DTW algorithm with various different constraints were used to evaluate 
against each other. 

The final result of the paper was that the method referred to as ``Sakoe-Chiba'' on 
the LPC performed the best with a 90\% agreement with the labels selected by human 
classifiers. 

This study did show that the use of DTW in bioacoustic analysis is 
a good option. However, it did not consider the ability of DTW to detect calls 
in a continuous segment of bioacoustic data as all calls which were classified had already 
been selected and segmented to the pitch contours of the vocalisation. This study also did 
not consider the impact of low frequency analysis using DTW as killer whale vocalisations 
do not tend to fall below 500 Hz.

\mysubsection{Analysis of Template-Based Detection Algorithms for Bryde's Whales}{Analysis of Template-Based Detection Algorithms for Bryde's Whales}\label{sec:bryde-dtw-lpc}

Ogundile and Versfeld analysed two template based detection algorithms to detect short pulse 
calls for Bryde's whales in False Bay, South Africa (see~\cite{temp-based-bryde}). The two methods tested were dynamic time warping (DTW) 
and linear predictive coding (LPC). In their analysis they used two separate sample sets with different signal-to-noise ratio (SNR) values 
to also evaluate the performance of these techniques in the presence of noise. The input 
features for these models were the time-domain representation of the signals. 

The final results of the article found that both LPC and DTW performed well in high 
and low SNR segments. LPC performed the overall best in low noise segments.

This article provides insight into the use of template based detection systems for 
Bryde's whales. This is relevant since DTW which is handled in this article is the chosen 
detection mechanism for this project. While LPC is also used in the article, its use is 
vastly different to the use within this project. LPC is used as a detection algorithm 
and tested on its ability to act as such. In this project LPC is not used as a detection 
algorithm, but instead linear predictive coding coefficients (LPCC) is used as a feature extraction method. 
The detection method focuses on 
the error component of the LPC equation while the feature extraction method focuses 
on the coefficients calculated for the approximation of the real signal.

\mysubsection{Detection of real-time fin and blue whale calls}{Detection of real-time fin and blue whale calls}\label{sec:real-time}

Mouy et al.~\cite{dtw-vq} tested detection models on 
real-time data for fin and blue whales. The 
detection methods used were dynamic time warping (DTW) and vector quantisation (VQ).

Instantaneous frequency, speed and acceleration were extracted as features. It was 
found that due to low frequency components from background elements such as ships the 
detection of false positives (FP) increased.

In terms of this project the article shows how low frequency detections can be affected 
by low frequency background noise. This article also considered real-time audio detection 
which is much closer in similarity to detection within a large audio file such as what was 
done in this project. This article did not use the same feature vectors as the project and 
did not consider their effect on DTW performance.

\mysubsection{Hidden Markov Model for Bryde's Whale short pulse calls}{Hidden Markov Model for Bryde's Whale short pulse calls}\label{sec:hmm-bryde}

Ogundile et al.~\cite{hmm} compared a time domain feature extraction technique used for hidden Markov models (HMM) against 
mel-frequency cepstral coefficients (MFCC) and linear predictive coding (LPC), frequency domain techniques. The model is used for detection of Bryde's 
whale calls in False Bay, South Africa.

These three HMM models are used on 2 hours of continuous recordings to detect these 
calls. This is similar to the continuous recording detection done in this project.

The time domain feature HMM model performed the best with MFCC performing second best and 
LPC performing the worst. It was found that the MFCC and LPC models were sensitive 
to background noise.

While this does test detection on a continuous segment of audio, this article focuses on 
HMM detection and not DTW. Similar to this project it compares the MFCC feature extraction method with the LPC method. 
However, considering the fact that the LPC features were not converted to 
linear predictive coding coefficients (LPCC) this does not fully align with this project.

\mysubsection{Speech Recognition using DTW}{Speech Recognition using DTW}\label{sec:speech-dtw}

Permanasari et al.~\cite{speech} analysed the effectiveness of dynamic time warping (DTW) in 
matching speech patterns. Variabilities were included in the study via pronunciation 
timing, volume and speaker identity. 

Features were extracted from the recorded audio. Some audio was used as templates 
and others as test data. DTW was then used to test which of the variations impacted 
DTW distances the most. The main tests were keeping the pronunciation time 
the same with the same volume, different pronunciation time with the same volume and 
same pronunciation time with different volume. 

It was determined that DTW was an effective pattern recognition algorithm and that 
pronunciation timing had a larger impact on DTW alignment than volume.

This study also uses DTW to recognise patterns in audio, however this focuses on 
speech recognition and the effects of variability on that recognition. It does not 
focus on different feature vectors or detection of low frequency whale calls in a continuous 
recording.
